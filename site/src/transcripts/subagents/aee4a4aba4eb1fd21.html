THE SIX READINGS, IN ORDER:

=== READING 1 ===

  1. PLOT: Cheis of Veraleigh, a blunt, self-deprecating necromancer with a computer/OS-metaphor magic system, is called to Morhelm to deal with a "curse" that turns out to be a buried Shul artifact; her containment attempt is sabotaged by a thrown rock and the whole village dies, though she survives, badly depleted. Meanwhile Linduin, a bookish enslaved-in-all-but-name teenage servant, is dragged along by the brutal tax-enforcer Tebes on a violent debt-collection circuit; they find an identical box in another obliterated village and loot its gold thread, unknowingly releasing something. In parallel, a modern-minded lich-bureaucrat, Velinaer, wakes from his sarcophagus in the wrong place/time, accidentally causes a small apocalypse in a fantasy city (echoing the same box), and tries to "call IT support." The threads converge in a tavern brawl where Cheis, escaped from bandits, kills Tebes via corpse-puppetry, befriends Linduin — and then the black "wisp" that escaped the second box reanimates Tebes' body and runs Cheis through, ending the chunk on a cliffhanger.

  2. NARRATION: Third-person past, close-omniscient but roving — each section (marked by *** breaks) locks tight to one POV (Cheis, Linduin, Velinaer, occasionally an unnamed "camera" like the Morhelm mob or Ciel-Upon-The-Sea). The narrator has a strong, dry authorial voice independent of any character — it editorializes freely ("Tebes was good at communication," "which is a far more common lesson for children to take to heart") and sometimes zooms out to mock-historical framing ("Like many things in the history of the human race..."). Character voice bleeds heavily into free indirect discourse, especially Cheis's clipped, deadpan interior monologue and Velinaer's Gen-X/office-worker cadence ("customer service skills abysmal"). POV shifts happen cleanly at scene breaks, never mid-scene, and the narrator often flash-forwards or foreshadows dryly ("If she'd known what awaited her, she might have stayed in bed").

  3. HUMOR MECHANISMS:

  • Deadpan understatement on atrocity: "That was definitely ranking very low on her list of Tuesdays."
  • Bathetic juxtaposition of horror and mundanity: "Cheis coughed, accidentally let out a fart, and looked around guiltily for witnesses before returning her gaze to the center of the runic circle."
  • Anachronistic register/jargon collision (magic-as-IT-support): "'LUMINEFEROUS ETHER NOT FOUND' did not inspire confidence."
  • Character-reaction comedy undercutting epic stakes: the lich's first coherent thought translated as "You have got to be fucking kidding me."
  • Escalating absurd catalogs/lists: Cheis's inventory of macros and "lie here and die" as a recurring refrain treated as a genuine cost-benefit option.
  • Aphoristic narratorial zingers: "The outcome was, when you think about it, inevitable."
    Density is high — probably 1 in 3-4 sentences carries a shaped joke or ironic beat, even in violent or grim passages; almost no stretch runs more than a paragraph as pure flat reporting.

  1. DARKNESS & STAKES: The book does not flinch from real horror — mass death (Morhelm, Ciel-Upon-The-Sea), torture (Tebes beating and maiming the headman), child-slavery undertones (Linduin), genuinely disturbing body horror (people "laughing and weeping from suppurating eyes as they tore at each other")—and lets the comedy sit right alongside it without undercutting the horror's reality, using comedy instead as the character's coping mechanism (Cheis's gallows deadpan) rather than as authorial denial. Example: "Around her, Morhelm was a charnel... The less said about what she dreamed, the better." — grim, unjoked-away final beat capping a chapter otherwise full of fart jokes.

  2. CHAPTER CRAFT: Chapters are titled with plain ordinal words (One, Two, Three...) and open in medias res with a scene-setting sentence, often ironic/formal in tone ("Of all his chores, Linduin Kayle disliked chopping firewood the least"). Scene breaks (***) are used liberally within chapters to crosscut between 2-3 POV threads, building dramatic irony (reader knows about the box before characters do). Chapters end on hard hooks or dark stingers:

  • "By the next sunset, every man, woman, and child in Morhelm would be dead."
  • "If she'd known what awaited her, however, she might have stayed in bed."
  • "The smile on Cheis' face abruptly changed into a slack-jawed expression of shock as Tebes' sword went straight through her chest."
    Pacing within chapters alternates fast violent action with slower, joke-dense interiority (especially Cheis's magic-system musing), giving comic relief valves between horror beats.
  1. EXEMPLAR PASSAGES:
  • "In another story, the book might have been a sorcerous tome from a lost age, pulsing with power and forgotten lore... but the reality is that it was a thoroughly non-magical book..." — shows the narrator's genre-savvy, subverting fantasy tropes directly while still building real backstory.
  • "Cheis, who had twice survived drinking poison and once defeated a ghost by biting it, was made of sufficiently stern material to survive the initial blast..." — the running technique of dropping absurd résumé-detail asides mid-sentence to characterize without slowing plot.
  • "Lie here and die: still not a bad plan. Had the benefit of having a 100% success chance with no meaningful side effects." — comic-listing voice used for genuine near-death despair, blending humor and pathos in one beat.
  • "But it should not be said that he did not have redeeming characteristics... he carried in his deepest heart of hearts a wistful longing for a girl he had once kissed at seventeen." — sudden humanizing aside for a monster right before he tries to kill the protagonist, complicating moral simplicity.
  • "After all, there's no rule that says you can't control a skeleton that someone else is currently using." — a one-line rules-lawyering punchline delivered as narration immediately after a brutal kill, characteristic voice-of-god dry aside.
  • "The wisp of blackness slithered forward another inch." (repeated refrain interleaved through the tavern fight) — a suspense-ticker technique borrowed from thriller pacing, crosscut against comic dialogue to build dread under the jokes.

  1. WEAKNESSES: The magic-system-as-tech-support conceit (thanatoptic buffer overflows, egregoric arrays, core emulator crashes) is clever once or twice but risks becoming a crutch/info-dump — long stretches of Cheis's interiority in chapter 4/5 read like a loot-menu UI description rather than prose, and could numb a reader unfamiliar with the joke's rules. The sheer density of quirky asides and mock-encyclopedic narrator intrusions ("Like many things in the history of the human race...") occasionally over-explains jokes or over-narrates transitions instead of trusting scene work — a Pratchett-adjacent risk of the narrator being funnier than necessary at the expense of momentum. Tebes' redemption-humanizing paragraph arrives so late (right before his death) it reads more as authorial cleverness than earned characterization. The rock-throw/coincidence engine (random villager sabotage causing catastrophe) is a slightly mechanical way to manufacture tragedy twice in close succession (Morhelm bandit-rock, box-thief villagers) — the coincidence machinery shows its gears.

  2. PROGRESSION/GENRE: Strong LitRPG/systems-fantasy DNA in Cheis's magic — literal inventory management, "macros," buffers, checksums, item purges, and cost/benefit item evaluation, played for comedy but functioning as real mechanical stakes (she has 4 usable macros, must ration them). Velinaer's arc plays magic-as-corporate-bureaucracy (performance reviews, error packets, calling his supervisor) rather than XP/leveling per se — more "magic as workplace" satire than power-progression fantasy. No explicit leveling/stat-screen shown yet, but the vocabulary primes readers for a systems-literate universe; this chunk sets up threads (the Shul boxes, Velinaer's error-packet paging system, Cheis's depleted toolkit) that clearly will matter mechanically later, suggesting slow-burn progression rather than immediate power-fantasy escalation.

=== READING 2 ===

  1. PLOT: Tebes' body is possessed/reanimated (a lich-empowered zombie), Cheis kills it magically and Linduin panics and beheads her with the cursed sword — accidentally freeing her soul, which ejects into astral projection while her body dies. She reboots herself via a "boot loader" ritual using a secret childhood diary, survives, and takes Linduin as an apprentice. Meanwhile the necromancer Velinaer (accidentally responsible, having killed the burgon in self-defense reflex) discovers he's become a lich, learns to manage his zombie horde and explore his own new undead body/magic. The corpse-blob "igg" that escaped from Tebes wanders off, eventually merges with a tree (the "Black Oak"), grows in intelligence, and spawns a demonic horde that devastates the region — Galar's home village is destroyed, his son is reported (wrongly) dead, and he's conscripted into the war effort as the human armies fight a losing battle against the oakspawn. Linduin spends months training under Cheis; Part One ends as she finally starts teaching him magic and Galar distinguishes himself in a brutal, doomed battle.

  2. NARRATION: Third-person omniscient with rapid, unmarked POV-hopping — often within a single scene (Cheis/Linduin/villager) or via a paragraph break to jump entire characters/locations (Galar, Velinaer, the igg). The narrator uses a distinctive move: stepping outside a scene to deliver deadpan world-building or backstory info-dumps (Tebes' sword history, the sign-of-the-star's true origin, the "counterfactual future" paragraph about what would have happened if the burgon's plans had succeeded) — often ironic, since the info immediately gets undercut by disaster. Free indirect style bleeds character idiom into narration constantly — Cheis's narration is littered with programmer/hacker jargon ("emulator," "boot loader," "kernel recompilation," "backup extractor") because that's literally how her magic system + personality work, so the narrative voice shifts vocabulary register depending on whose head we're in. The narrator also editorializes with an almost mock-scholarly formality ("It should be noted at this point that...", "Firstly... Secondly... Thirdly...") that contrasts with grotesque or crude content.

  3. HUMOR MECHANISMS:

  • Escalating clinical/technical description of horror ("a number of interesting chemical, electrical, and astral changes were taking place... the corpse began to shit its own bones out of its asshole") — grotesque body horror rendered in bureaucratic prose.
  • Deadpan understatement: "This was going to be an interesting conversation." / "This was not a fortunate turn of events, and had a large number of consequences."
  • Character-reaction comedy: the drunk villager who "excused himself with great politeness before vomiting copiously."
  • Bathos/anticlimax: Velinaer, a lich capable of destroying a kingdom, is mostly occupied with feeling awkward, wanting to be clean, and going "FUCKING AWESOME!" during a fight.
  • Wordplay/logic humor: Linduin's absurd decision-tree analysis of "hit yourself with a rock," ending in a bucket-assisted technicality.
  • Anachronistic register clash: fantasy setting narrated with modern slang/tech jargon ("without a boot loader her emulator wasn't coming back," "totally sweet staff moves," "catastro-fuck").
  • Numbered/listed structural jokes ("Firstly, the burgon died instantly... Secondly... Thirdly... Fourthly, Velinaer's already tenuous sanity took another hit.")
    DENSITY: very high — nearly every paragraph carries at least one shaped joke or wry aside; plain reporting is rare and usually reserved for battle/grief scenes (file0012's marshal scene, the battle in file0013) where jokes drop out almost entirely.

  1. DARKNESS & STAKES: The book toggles hard between slapstick/gross-out and real grief without much softening — Galar's wife's memory (the painting he can't look at, then forces himself to leave) and his son's false death notification are played completely straight, no jokes in that scene at all. The war/massacre reporting (Morhelm "no survivors, not even women and children") is blunt and unfunny. But the humor infrastructure (Cheis's near-death, Velinaer's killing of the burgon) treats death as absurdist plot mechanics even while character consequences (grief, trauma, guilt) are taken seriously in POV-adjacent characters. Example: "Robard Pols gave his life to protect his citizens. I want that on the record." — flat, dignified, no punchline, sitting right next to Velinaer's whimsy scenes two pages earlier.

  2. CHAPTER CRAFT: Chapters open in medias res or with a short character-orienting line ("Galar Kayle sighed. It wasn't even noon, and this was already proving to be the worst day of his week."). Endings are often quiet character-note landings or ironic reversal punchlines:

  • "Behind him, the trapdoor slammed and locked with a crash." (Ch.9)
  • "Outside the tavern, some distance away, the blob of black slime shuddered a bit more, and grew a tentacle with an eye on the end of it." (Ch.7, ominous coda after a comic-grotesque climax)
  • "Cheis of Veraleigh 4.0 was no more." (Ch.10, deadpan cliffhanger)
    Scene breaks (***) are used heavily within chapters to crosscut between 3-4 storylines (Cheis/Linduin, Galar, Velinaer, the igg) — pacing is brisk, alternating comic beats with escalating plot movement, and each thread gets planted early then paid off later in the same chunk.
  1. EXEMPLAR PASSAGES:
  • "Cheis, who had been to the horizon and left a two-star review, wanted a chocolate cake." — compresses a whole worldview/character contrast (Linduin's naive wonder vs. Cheis's jaded practicality) into one perfectly balanced sentence.
  • "The one good thing about the current situation was that it allowed him to vent his frustrations often and vigorously upon the heads of those who gained his ire... The villagers... were beginning to wear hats despite the unseasonably warm weather, purely for the protection against drubbings they provided." — classic Pratchett-style indirect characterization via absurd consequence.
  • "He grabbed rations, a refillable canteen for water, and a traveling cloak... He couldn't let those fall to the enemy." followed by the painting scene — shows the book's real emotional register: comic bureaucratic efficiency abruptly interrupted by grief, without tonal whiplash because the prose stays controlled.
  • "I don't give partial credit. A functional demonstration of how to cheat like a bastard while still solving the problem is nice and all, but you still need to show your work for it to qualify as a general solution." — mentor voice nails comic warmth + thematic statement (this book cares about cleverness and rigor) in one line.
  • "Cheis of Veraleigh 4.0 was no more." — chapter-ending punch that's both a joke (software versioning) and genuinely dramatic (is she dead?).
  • "No oakspawn which came near him escaped." — short, hard sentence dropped after a long grim battle paragraph, showing the prose can turn off the jokes entirely for grief-fueled competence porn.

  1. WEAKNESSES: The tech/programmer jargon for magic (bootloader, emulator, kernel recompilation, cacopneuma infection, "she wasn't ruling out betrayal") is a fun conceit but risks overstaying — in file0011 nearly every paragraph of Cheis's recovery leans on it, and it can read as a bit indulgent/gimmicky if it doesn't evolve. The grotesque body-horror descriptions (zombie transformation, "shit its own bones out of its asshole") are very funny once but the well could run dry with repetition — worth noting as a device to ration. Some infodump paragraphs (Tebes' sword backstory, the sign-of-the-star history) are delightful but static — they stop scene momentum cold for a paragraph of essayistic backstory, which is a technique to use sparingly. The prose is occasionally overwritten/run-on (long comma-spliced sentences describing battle mechanics or magical body horror) which could read as try-hard if not for the jokes.

  2. PROGRESSION/GENRE: Explicit LitRPG/hacker-culture magic system for Cheis specifically (not universal) — spells as "macros," memory as "backups," consciousness as "emulator" needing a "boot loader," with real permadeath stakes tied to system corruption ("cacopneuma infection"). This is unusual and distinctive: it's diegetic (a personal magical philosophy/technology Cheis built, written in a childhood diary) rather than a game-UI overlay imposed on the whole world — other characters (Galar, the marshal, villagers) experience magic as straightforwardly supernatural/mysterious, not systematized. Linduin's "sums"/math aptitude as the seed of a magical education (echoing programmer-logic magic) sets up an apprenticeship arc that reads like a training montage (algebra→calculus in weeks) compressed comically fast — a genre convention (fast-tracked chosen-one training) played partly for laughs but taken seriously as foreshadowing his eventual competence. No stat blocks or numeric leveling shown yet in this chunk.

=== READING 3 ===

  1. PLOT: Velinaer the accidental lich slowly refines his skeletal body and clothing, builds a signal tower to call for help — which instead broadcasts chaos worldwide and draws the Black Oak/igg directly to him, destroying his zombie minions and absorbing the tower before striding off, leaving Velinaer alive by pure luck. Cheis begins training Linduin in earnest (critical-thinking philosophy of magic, a life-or-death "which rune" test, the magic-word collar/stock, and a lecture on mana/souls/telekinesis), then Cheis's ex-boyfriend Pellamin arrives to recruit her against a Shul-contagion outbreak in Ciel-Upon-The-Sea, and the trio travels there by coach and ship. In parallel, Galar Kayle survives a last-stand battle at Saurgar Bridge by accidentally manifesting the "White Gift," becomes a reluctant paladin/political asset for Princess Orána, and gives a stage-managed rallying speech to recruit soldiers.

  2. NARRATION: Close third limited that hops between four POV characters (Velinaer, Linduin, Cheis, Galar) at scene breaks (***), never mid-scene. The narrator is deeply infected by whichever character it's tracking — Velinaer's sections use programmer/sysadmin jargon ("error messages," "core exception," "recovery attempts," "task runner") as his literal cognitive idiom, not just metaphor; Cheis's sections carry her profanity and bitterness even in indirect discourse ("Then maybe she shouldn't have fucked my boyfriend, Cheis did not say"). The narrator frequently makes omniscient, slightly essayistic asides that step back from any one character to deliver a wry generalization ("One of the problems with the concept of division of labor..."), then snaps back into scene. Free indirect style is heavy — thoughts render as narration without tags ("Frickin' nasty," "Whoa. Awesome.").

  3. HUMOR MECHANISMS:

  • Register clash/anachronism: "Velinaer wasted no time creating and executing his plans... tested them using a crude signal attenuation simulator" — high fantasy body horror described in software-engineering register.
  • Understatement/deflating epic stakes: "'I,' he intoned, very solemnly, 'am not paid nearly enough for this shit.'" after apocalyptic carnage.
  • Escalating absurd lists: "recalled, commended, blamed, threatened with dishonor, found to possess blackmail material, and quietly reassigned" — bureaucratic escalation applied to war.
  • Character-reaction comedy: Cheis's whacking pedagogy ("Magic is bullshit... Repeat it") and Linduin's naive one-liners ("It sounds like you've given me a magic item that lets me shock myself, but with extra steps").
  • Bathos via mundane detail: apocalyptic magical fallout undercut by punchline ("A few craftsmen in Moratrakashi went insane... but in Moratrakashi this barely raised an eyebrow").
  • Meta/genre-joke: "Linduin discovered... that 3 * 0.1 was somehow 0.30000000000000004" — a floating-point joke dropped into fantasy prose.
    Density is high — probably 60-70% of sentences carry some shaping (wit, irony, escalation), especially in Velinaer and Cheis/Linduin sections; the Galar/Orána political sections are noticeably drier and more purely plot/character, with humor thinner and more situational (comedy of manners rather than line-level wit).

  1. DARKNESS & STAKES: Real death and horror sit right beside jokes without either undercutting the other — child skeletons being "deanimated" as "too small to be useful, and creepy besides" is genuinely eerie and funny at once. Linduin's life-or-death rune test is played completely straight for tension (real fear, real high stakes, real threat of death) with comedy only in the after-relief ("My desire to fuck around has indeed been greatly reduced, I assure you"). Galar's suicidal last stand at Saurgar Bridge is unironic, moving, mythic in register — the comedy fully drops out for these passages. Cheis's backstory ("I had to clean bits of her off the ceiling" re: an apprentice who exploded) delivers real horror as a punchline, showing the book's signature move: horror and joke occupy the same sentence without diluting either.

  2. CHAPTER CRAFT: Chapters open in medias res on a character's interior state or immediate situation ("Velinaer was profoundly, recursively entranced," "When Galar Kayle awoke, he thought that he was dead") and use *** scene breaks liberally within chapters to hop POV/time/location, sometimes multiple times per chapter. Endings favor a hard tonal button — often a punchline or ominous reveal:

  • "And so, on the whole, things were much less horrific than they might have been." (undercutting dread with dry relief)
  • "'I,' he intoned, very solemnly, 'am not paid nearly enough for this shit.'" (bathos after carnage)
  • "'Hello, Umbria,' said Cheis, wishing she were dead. It might have cheered her up to know that within seven days, one of them would be." (ominous foreshadow hook)
    Pacing within chapters is brisk, summary-heavy in places (skipping weeks/months of training or campaign in a paragraph) then slowing dramatically into scene for emotionally charged beats (the rune test, the speech).
  1. EXEMPLAR PASSAGES:
  • "He'd learned enough to make a run at it, at least... he set up a redundant series of backups, carefully copied-and-pasted the necessary components of his body code into several editors for maximum fallback capability" — exemplifies the book's core comic engine: total tonal commitment to treating necromancy as software engineering, sustained across paragraphs rather than as a one-off gag.
  • "'Magic is bullshit,' droned Linduin obligingly. 'But that doesn't answer my question!'" — shows the book teaching real-world epistemics (skepticism, cognitive bias) through slapstick, Pratchett's Granny Weatherwax mode of "headology" filtered through a rationalist lens.
  • "Please, let me have a second chance at getting you killed? Please, give up your hot sexy girlfriend for me, a dumpy, uncultured psychopath who risks her life out of boredom and self-aggrandizement?" — internal monologue self-deprecation that's funny and genuinely sad simultaneously, very Bujold in its emotional honesty about an unglamorous protagonist.
  • "The metal shaft of his silver spear, which was in no way magical or sacred, functioned as a suitable enough conductor and antenna... took on a rather different modulation." — miracle explained via circuit-diagram logic, keeping the "magic system" rigorously mechanistic even at its most transcendent moment.
  • "This was, of course, completely incorrect." — one-line chapter-ending zinger, deadpan dramatic irony pointed straight at the reader.
  • "Galar Kayle... was perhaps the worst person in the entire kingdom of Temurin at admitting fault... But he was, in fact, very good at solving problems." — efficient, funny, and touching characterization in a single compact list.

  1. WEAKNESSES: The prose sometimes over-explains its own jokes and world mechanics — the "explanatory pamphlet" gag about the lich system, the "ulasovs" energy-unit riff, and long process-narration of Velinaer's tinkering can run long and read as padding rather than momentum; a few paragraphs (e.g., the extended suspension-fluid engineering passage) indulge in worldbuilding detail past the point where the joke lands. Some sentences are baggy/run-on with piled-up subordinate clauses that dilute punchlines rather than sharpen them. The dialogue occasionally tips into having characters explain the theme to each other quite directly (Cheis's "cost of magic," Galar's "division of labor" essay-paragraph) — useful for a serial audience tracking rules but risks didacticism an author aiming for tighter Pratchett-style economy should watch. The sexual-tension/romance beats (Pellamin/Cheis/Umbria) lean on fairly familiar love-triangle mechanics without much freshness compared to the sharper comic and magic-system material.

  2. PROGRESSION/GENRE: Explicit LitRPG-adjacent mechanics throughout — Velinaer's status commands, error messages, numbered energy units (ulasovs), Y/N prompts for his minions ("CONSUME PROXIMATE BRAINS? Y/N"), and Linduin's collar with named keyword-triggered "functions" and a training regimen with literal "high scores" (nine seconds, three hours). Magic is presented as a rigorously specified system (mana as fungible resource with sourcing/cost tradeoffs, souls as reproducible "patterns," runes as arbitrary trigger-tokens) taught pedagogically to an audience surrogate (Linduin), very much in the "hard magic system" tradition (Sanderson-adjacent) crossed with programming/systems-administration flavor for the lich content specifically. This chunk is mid-progression: Linduin is explicitly leveling up (first successful telekinesis, first real spell), Galar is discovering the limits and shape of his new power in real time through trial and error, and Velinaer's power growth is largely accidental/emergent rather than earned, which comically undercuts the genre's usual power-fantasy arc.

=== READING 4 ===

  1. PLOT: Drunk Cheis lets slip clues about her origins to Linduin (her "compiler" was created young by casting a mind-altering spell on herself). Linduin secretly duplicates the spell, is attacked by a rakshasi's (Nyoque's) thralls at the library, and kills one with a lethal collar failsafe. Meanwhile Galar's army finally engages the Black Oak igg at Craflexe Pass, Velinaer (aided by zombie-companion Meloria, once Galar's wife) arrives and cripples it with an engineering "removal command," and the igg's core launches into the sea to regenerate and continue toward the "signal." Nyoque orchestrates an elaborate honey-trap dinner (all courses secretly alcoholic) that knocks out Cheis, Pellamin, and Linduin, delivering Cheis to torturers who mutilate and magically silence her, and staging a compromising bedroom scene between Linduin and Umbria. Linduin deduces the setup and flees rather than falling for it, then does the mind-spell a second time (permanently tattooing the ritual on himself), which strips his empathy and turns him into a hyper-competent, narcissistic combat-planner; Cheis "reboots" from a two-week-old backup to survive the torture, losing memories, and both converge — visibly transformed — on the archmage's office. Nyoque, meanwhile, recruits a dim god-like vanoille as muscle using Cheis's stolen anklet as tribute, and the igg regenerates at sea and starts destroying ships as it heads toward the same signal.

  2. NARRATION: Close free-indirect third, hopping POV character-to-character within and across scene breaks (Linduin, Cheis, Galar, Velinaer, Nyoque, even the igg's alien cognition) with no formal marker beyond a *** break. The narrator maintains an urbane, essayistic, slightly Pratchett-lecturing voice ("It is natural but wrong to imagine...", "One of the most effective strategies in the great game of intrigue is...") that sits above all characters, but freely dips into a character's diction inside that frame — Velinaer's sections shift into blunt modern slang ("big-ass body of water," "Cool Staff Guy," "What the fuck was everyone else doing?") entirely unmarked by quotation, while Cheis's internal narration uses her clipped, profane, tech-inflected idiom even in third person ("Take that, whoever the fuck you were"). This bleed is the book's signature device: narration voice = whoever's head we're currently in, filtered through one authorial register.

  3. HUMOR MECHANISMS:

  • Anachronistic-diction collision (mundane modern phrasing draped over epic fantasy): "he could, he felt, be seen by others without fatal levels of embarrassment" describing a demon lord fretting about his zombie-mount's dignity.
  • Deadpan escalation/bathos: "All right. Now she was pissed." — one-line chapter-closer after horrific torture description.
  • Technical/LitRPG jargon comedy: "her energy levels... were at a downright measly 648%; she really needed to get around to refactoring the scale on that."
  • Character-reaction understatement: "Uh oh." (Linduin, waking naked next to Umbria).
  • Misnaming/mishearing jokes: Velinaer dubbing the vanoille "Lord Ivan Wall" from "I VANOILLE."
  • Wildly literalized cliché/action-scene parody undercut by narrator irony: the absurdly over-the-top fight choreography in office lobby scene, played completely straight in prose but tonally absurd.
    Density: fairly high but front-loaded into narrator asides and one-liners rather than every sentence; roughly 1 in 4-5 sentences carries a shaped joke or ironic turn, with long stretches of plain battle/plot reporting (especially igg/Galar war material) that are largely unshaped, functional prose.

  1. DARKNESS & STAKES: Genuinely brutal — graphic torture (spikes through palms, severed toes, forced magical muteness), death of dozens of nameless soldiers/civilians, demon predation, a near-rape setup engineered by a manipulative demon, and real emotional/psychological cost (Linduin lobotomizing his own empathy; Cheis losing two weeks of memory and having to "reboot"). The comedy doesn't undercut the horror so much as run in a parallel channel — Cheis's torture is described in unflinching physical detail, then immediately followed by her irritated, deadpan problem-solving voice ("her first thought was irritation that whoever had crippled and tortured her had probably stolen her good shoes, too"). This coexistence (horror rendered straight, character response rendered funny/practical) is the book's central tonal trick — it reads as coping/competence-under-duress humor rather than a joke made at the trauma's expense.

  2. CHAPTER CRAFT: Chapters open in medias res or with a dry expository/character-assessment paragraph (Ch 22 opens "Linduin Kayle was not, by any stretch of the imagination, a genius."). Endings favor a hard cut on a punchy short sentence or ironic reveal: "END OF PART TWO" (structural), "All right. Now she was pissed." (Ch 24), "Everything which followed, naturally, was inevitable." (Ch 26, ominous authorial foreshadow). Scene breaks (***) are used heavily and rapidly within chapters to crosscut between 3-4 simultaneous plotlines (Linduin/library, Galar's battle, Cheis/dinner, Velinaer), building tension via interleaving rather than long unbroken scenes — pacing is brisk, almost episodic-TV in rhythm.

  3. EXEMPLAR PASSAGES:

  • "It was deeply unfair, in Cheis's opinion, that Umbria DaMoura was allowed to be beautiful, sexy, kind, graceful, and efficient, and also be a good cook." — instant character voice + comic economy opening a chapter.
  • "That's my nickname for him, you bitch, she thought, but managed to keep a rictus vaguely resembling a smile on her face." — perfect comic gap between interior fury and exterior composure, classic slice-of-life-with-teeth.
  • "Cheis's protective wards, which could neutralize any poison, did not register ethyl hydroxide as hazardous in any way" — worldbuilding joke (magic vs. mundane loophole) doing plot work and humor simultaneously.
  • "She loaded a prior checkpoint, ran a quick checksum, and applied it with the '-s' and '--body' flags; her vision dimmed for a moment... Damn. Now she had to wash her hair again." — the book's core comic register: catastrophic magic described as software ops, punctured by a mundane petty complaint.
  • "His most expert cognitive talent, like many intelligent people, was the ability to analyze exactly how far he fell short of his own concept of intellect." — Pratchett/Bujold-style aphoristic character summary, generalizes outward then lands specifically.
  • "Was he, as usual, the only competent engineer around here?" — Velinaer's voice fully colonizing third-person narration, comic through registration mismatch with epic-fantasy setting.

  1. WEAKNESSES: The battle-sequence prose (Galar vs. igg, Velinaer's tech-jargon spellcasting) runs long and can feel like padding — dense with invented terminology and mechanical blow-by-blow that slows momentum without much comic or emotional payoff; risks LitRPG-fatigue for readers not invested in the systems. The office-lobby fight in Ch27 is an extended action set-piece played completely straight for several paragraphs of choreography — it's meant to land as absurdist overkill via cumulative excess, but it goes on long enough that the joke (this is absurdly over-the-top) risks blurring into just being an action scene the author enjoyed writing. Occasional overuse of italicized profanity/crude asides ("big-ass," "shit," "fuck") for Velinaer risks feeling like a one-note voice rather than a fully developed character. The information-dense worldbuilding paragraphs (rakshasi/vanoille/danava taxonomy) occasionally read as lore-dump rather than scene.

  2. PROGRESSION/GENRE: Strong, explicit LitRPG-adjacent framing for Cheis's magic: numeric "power levels" (as percentages, e.g. "648%", "10k%"), "checkpoints"/save-states she can revert to, macros, compiler/lexer metaphors, command-line syntax for spells ("-s --mind --force -y"). This is played simultaneously straight (functional magic system with real stakes — she loses memories from an old backup) and comedically (jargon deployed for bathos, e.g., "she really needed to get around to refactoring the scale on that"). Progression is not XP-grinding in the traditional sense but a hacker/engineer paradigm of magic — Linduin's arc this chunk is a literal skill-unlock (Stosser's Keening) with real psychological cost, echoing the "power has a price" trope used sincerely rather than for pure game-mechanic pleasure. Velinaer's magic is explicitly framed as "teledemonics engineering" — sysadmin/network-engineer competence porn transplanted into a fantasy war, comic through the sheer mismatch of register but genuinely functional as a plot-solving tool (his "null-phasic" attack turns the tide of the whole battle).

=== READING 5 ===

  1. PLOT: Cheis, accused of treason by the Ciel'sa Arcane Council (secretly manipulated by the rakshasi Nyoque), fights and slaughters much of the council, including a metaphysical duel with Archmage Ulbert that nearly kills her. Meanwhile Linduin has been covertly hunting Nyoque via his emotionless "compiled" combat-mode and finally unmasks and fights it, defeating it (with his father Galar's help) just as, across the harbor, Velinaer chases the kaiju-esque "igg" to the last teledemonics node and sacrifices his sanity/reserves to seal a dimensional breach, devastating the city. Cheis, Linduin, and Galar reunite (father-son reveal played for comedy/pathos), realize a Shul-era lich (revealed as Velinaer, cosplaying as a villain out of loneliness/despair) is behind the demon-summoning, and the chunk ends as the party engages Velinaer in a fight where he's secretly pulling punches and increasingly infatuated with Cheis. This is chunk 5/6, so climax convergence of three previously-separate plotlines (Cheis/council, Linduin/Nyoque, Velinaer/igg) is clearly the structural point of this chunk.

  2. NARRATION: Close third omniscient with hard, unmarked head-hops between scenes and even within scenes/sentences (e.g., switching from Cheis's tactical POV to Ulbert's backstory mid-paragraph). Scene breaks (***) cut between the three converging threads almost cinematically, escalating tension via cross-cutting. The narrator's voice constantly absorbs the POV character's idiom and register — Velinaer's chapters shift into gamer/tech-bro internal voice ("This was super, mega, ultra-hyper-giga-fucking-zetta-goddamn bullshit. He wanted his mother."), Cheis's into profane bravado, Nyoque's into theatrical melodrama — so free indirect discourse is the dominant technique. The narrator also editorializes with dry omniscient asides ("Some shit, as they say, was about to go down") that sit above all POVs, giving a Pratchett-ish narrator-as-character layer.

  3. HUMOR MECHANISMS:

  • Anachronistic-register clash/bathos: "Cheis laughed as the motes struck her; her power reserves jumped up to 12,848%... It was like having a denial-of-service attack levied against you by written mail."
  • Understatement/deflation after horror: "he survived, but wished very much that he hadn't."
  • Escalating absurd compounding (piling qualifiers): "This was super, mega, ultra-hyper-giga-fucking-zetta-goddamn bullshit."
  • Character-reaction jokes undercutting epic action: Nyoque distracted mid-fight by Linduin grabbing its butt: "'A pity,' hissed Nyoque. 'This was almost a -- Uwah!'"
  • Deadpan cataloguing/lists: Cheis ticking off the day's disasters on her fingers ("a rakshasi... a vanoille... a demon king in the harbor, and we killed half the council--").
  • Meta/genre-aware jokes: Velinaer narrating his own villain cosplay ("Look, mom, I'm a superhero," "Okay. Now it was just him and black-robe hacker.").
  • Naming-gag humor: nicknames like "Sleepy Ninja Guy," "Glowing Spear Boy," "Cool Staff Guy."
    Density is high — probably 1 in 3-4 sentences carries a shaped joke or ironic beat, even inside major action/death scenes; almost no purely flat expository sentence goes unturned for long before a wink.

  1. DARKNESS & STAKES: Genuine horror sits right alongside jokes without tonal warning — witnesses thrown out windows to their screaming deaths, a man's bones shattered by a lightning bolt to the groin, Cheis clinically dying for nearly four minutes, mass civilian casualties from the breach closure — but these are delivered in the same wry, escalating voice as the comedy, e.g. "It smashed Pellamin backwards against the wall painfully, stripped off all the wards of every member of the council, and blasted two of the five witnesses out the windows of the room. They fell seven floors, screaming and bleeding from glass lacerations; Cheis didn't really think of bureaucrats as people." The joke ("didn't really think of bureaucrats as people") sits directly against real deaths, forcing the reader to hold both. Velinaer's loneliness/grief (realizing his entire civilization and language are gone) is played almost entirely straight and is the darkest emotional beat in the chunk, undercut only slightly by "he'd never get to watch another show or play another video game for all eternity" — comedy as coping mechanism, not erasure of the grief.

  2. CHAPTER CRAFT: Chapters open in medias res on a POV thread already in motion (no throat-clearing), use *** scene breaks liberally within chapters to cross-cut between the 2-3 simultaneous plotlines, and close on cliffhangers or ironic buttons. Example endings: "For a long moment, neither of them moved. Then, slowly, Nyoque's gaze rose up to meet Linduin's. 'Not so pure of heart after all, it would seem.' Linduin gulped. This was probably going to suck." / "'Good sir and madam,' said Nyoque dramatically, 'may I introduce you to my acquaintance, the good Lord Ivan Wall?'" (revealing the vanoille as chapter-end shock). / "He extended his animatory will down through the rubble below him... he summoned up a suitably ominous red glow about himself to put a proper mood on things. Look at me, I'm evil. Come and get me." Pacing is breathless within chapters — battles are staged almost blow-by-blow with heavy technical/magical jargon, then punctured by a one-line joke to reset tension before the next beat.

  3. EXEMPLAR PASSAGES:

  • "These fucking bumpkins, with their geometric constructs! It was like having a denial-of-service attack levied against you by written mail." — perfectly compresses Cheis's power-scaling contempt into one anachronistic simile, doing exposition and character and joke simultaneously.
  • "Velinaer himself, who naturally survived almost completely unscathed, struggled to the top of a tremendous pile of debris and stared out at the wreckage and ruin stretching nearly to the horizon in every direction. In his terminal, a small alert appeared, informing him that his power reserves had dipped to 3.19 ulasovs." — the flat status-readout after mass devastation is the book's signature LitRPG-deadpan move, letting the UI element do the emotional work of horror-via-understatement.
  • "He was alone -- completely, irreversibly, and utterly alone, and also naked since his clothes hadn't survived the devastation -- and the only person who was going to praise him was himself." — the comma splice tacking bathetic "also naked" onto genuine existential grief is the whole book's tonal method in miniature.
  • "But, at the end of the day, the facts were inescapable: when all one has is a hammer, one tends to perceive the universe primarily as an environment which allows one to interact with nails. Peace never stood a chance." — a chapter-closing aphorism that's simultaneously a joke and real characterization/theme statement, very Pratchett.
  • "Cheis, who was standing back a bit and observing their foe... Wow, look at that instruction density. Was that a recursively encrypted command exchange stack? She added more wards to herself, feeling underdressed." — magic-as-programming register used for both worldbuilding and a self-deprecating joke ("feeling underdressed") in one beat.
  • Dialogue: "'You killed a rakshasi using tricks you cobbled together from your training item?' Cheis was impressed, and that did not happen often. 'You've got potential, kid. Maybe I'll make a good apprentice out of you yet.'" — mentor-voice warmth delivered dryly, no sentimentality, immediately followed by practical danger-warning, which is the book's warmth-with-teeth mode.

  1. WEAKNESSES: The tech/programming jargon is laid on extremely thick in fight scenes ("floating-point underflow," "recursively-encrypted command exchange stack," "malformed packet into the kid's data processing pipeline") to the point of overwhelming legibility of the actual physical action — a reader can lose track of who's winning a fight under the joke-density and metaphor-stacking. Battle sequences run long and blow-by-blow, and the constant tonal deflation of every death (even innocent bystanders/civilians) risks numbing stakes if overused — the "Apecis... had not so much perished as questionably existed in the first place" line is funny but also a slightly glib dismissal of mass death that a more restrained author might cut. Numbers/statistics-as-jokes (12,848%, 5.617e9%, 3.19 ulasovs) are a good bit but used repeatedly enough to become a slightly predictable tic. The father-son Galar/Linduin reunion is compressed almost too fast into banter, undercutting potential emotional payoff.

  2. PROGRESSION/GENRE: Strong LitRPG-adjacent framing: literal percentage power reserves, "compiled"/scripted spell-casting language, HUD timers, status-alert popups ("CONTAINMENT FAILURE SCENARIO 999," "power reserves had dipped to 3.19 ulasovs," "UNAUTHORIZED CONTACT: CONSUME PARTICIPANT? Y/N"), and Velinaer's death-magic explicitly coded as hacking/networking/sysadmin work (teledemonics as literal IT infrastructure, "root password," "line-of-sight sanitization," malformed-packet attacks). This is used consistently as a character-voice/worldbuilding device rather than mechanical stat-porn — it characterizes Velinaer as an anxious IT professional out of his depth and gives Cheis's power a hard, funny, legible scaling logic, and it's deployed for humor (self-aware boss-fight staging: "The heroes had to fight their way through minions... Look at me, I'm evil. Come and get me.") more than for crunchy progression-tracking.

No explicit XP/leveling on the page here, but the framing of combat as protocol-exploitation (probes, honeypots, exploits, permissions) is the book's central genre-blend device, played consistently for both comedy and, in the "I'm so sorry" beat, real emotional stakes.

Avaunt — Full-Book Craft Study

Source: Avaunt by AMBLE (~84k words, Royal Road; comic high fantasy with a diegetic tech-magic system). Synthesized from six sequential close readings of the whole book. Companion to avaunt-influence.md (prologue/ch.1 close read), voices.md, and llm-tells.md.

1. Plot and arc, compactly

Three strangers' disasters converge. Cheis of Veraleigh — blunt, plain, self-deprecating necromancer whose personal magic system is literally software (macros, backups, emulators, a childhood-diary boot loader) — botches the containment of a buried Shul artifact when a thrown rock sabotages her ritual; a village dies. Linduin Kayle, a bookish quasi-enslaved teenager, is dragged on a debt-collection circuit by the brutal Tebes, loots an identical box, and releases the black "igg." Velinaer, a Shul-era lich-bureaucrat with the soul of an anxious modern IT professional, wakes millennia out of place, accidentally levels a city, and spends the book trying to reach tech support.

The threads braid: Cheis kills Tebes, is killed and reboots herself, takes Linduin as apprentice. The igg merges with a tree, spawns a demon war; Linduin's father Galar accidentally becomes a paladin. In the city of Ciel: a rakshasi's intrigue plot tortures Cheis (she reboots from a two-week-old backup) and drives Linduin to self-lobotomize his empathy for competence via a mind-altering spell. Climax convergence: Cheis slaughters the manipulated Arcane Council, Linduin kills the rakshasi with his father's help, Velinaer seals the dimensional breach at catastrophic cost — then, revealed as the "villain" (cosplaying evil out of cosmic loneliness), throws his final duel with Cheis so she can destroy him. She resurrects him instead. Epilogue: political fallout in fairy-tale-historian register; Cheis and Velinaer share a long platonic afterlife; the book ends on a god telling humanity to "read the FAQ, noobs."

Arc shape: three separate disaster-origins → braided escalation with heavy dramatic irony (reader always knows more than any character) → mid-book personal costs (torture, empathy-loss, grief) → convergent climax where every thread's system knowledge is load-bearing → deliberately anticlimactic warm landing.

2. How the narrative voice works

The engine is free indirect discourse wearing an omniscient narrator's coat. Third person past, one POV per ***-marked section (early book; later it hops mid-scene and even mid-paragraph). Two layers:

  1. A constant authorial narrator — dry, mock-scholarly, opinionated. It editorializes ("Tebes was good at communication"), delivers essayistic asides ("One of the problems with the concept of division of labor..."), flash-forwards ("By the next sunset, every man, woman, and child in Morhelm would be dead"), and drops counterfactual paragraphs about futures that won't happen.
  2. Total colonization by the current POV's idiom. Near Cheis, narration goes clipped, profane, programmer-inflected ("Then maybe she shouldn't have fucked my boyfriend, Cheis did not say"). Near Velinaer it goes tech-bro ("this chick really was a hacker pro," "big-ass body of water"). Near Galar it goes plain, dutiful, almost mythic. Near the igg it goes alien. The vocabulary of the narration is the character's cognition — crucially, the jargon isn't authorial flavor; it's diegetic, because Cheis and Velinaer literally think that way and nobody else in the world does.

Evolution across the book: the mock-encyclopedic narrator asides dominate early (Pratchett-lecture mode); as the cast deepens, the narrator recedes and the character-idiom bleed becomes the primary voice device; the final duel alternates "To Cheis... / To Velinaer..." framings inside a single scene, then pulls back to bystanders in the water to reframe the spectacle as unknowable from outside; the epilogue shifts wholesale into compressed fairy-tale-historian register ("The rest, as they say, was history"). So the voice's identity is stable but its distance is an instrument played across the whole book.

Best single evidence of the method: the narrator can deliver, inside one register, both "It should be noted at this point that..." pedantry and "You have got to be fucking kidding me" — and the reader never feels a seam, because the seam is the joke.

3. Taxonomy of the humor

Ordered roughly by load-bearing weight:

  1. Register clash / anachronism (the core engine). High fantasy carnage in sysadmin diction. "It was like having a denial-of-service attack levied against you by written mail." "CONSUME PROXIMATE BRAINS? Y/N." This is one conceit sustained for 84k words; it works because it's a character's worldview, not narrator decoration.
  2. Deadpan understatement after horror. "That was definitely ranking very low on her list of Tuesdays." "This was not a fortunate turn of events, and had a large number of consequences." Chapter-closer variant: "All right. Now she was pissed." — after graphic torture.
  3. Bathos via equal-weight lists. Apocalypse and trivia itemized identically: "He'd gotten to eat a lot of cereal. Watched his favorite shows. Won that calligraphy competition at nine. Saved the world and/or universe."
  4. Character-reaction comedy. The gap between event scale and response scale: a lich's first coherent thought translating as "You have got to be fucking kidding me"; Linduin waking naked next to Umbria: "Uh oh."
  5. Mock-scholarly narrator machinery. Numbered consequences ("Firstly, the burgon died instantly... Fourthly, Velinaer's already tenuous sanity took another hit"), fake etymology ("In the coming years 'to turn on the crown' became an idiom for doing something extremely and suicidally stupid"), genre-aware trope subversion ("In another story, the book might have been a sorcerous tome...").
  6. Grotesque-clinical description. Body horror in bureaucratic prose ("a number of interesting chemical, electrical, and astral changes were taking place..."). Funny once or twice per book; the well is shallow.
  7. UI-readout deadpan. The status line as punchline and gut-punch: after citywide devastation, "a small alert appeared, informing him that his power reserves had dipped to 3.19 ulasovs."
  8. Absurd résumé asides. "Cheis, who had twice survived drinking poison and once defeated a ghost by biting it..." — characterization dropped mid-sentence without slowing plot.
  9. Naming gags and mishearings. "Lord Ivan Wall" (from "I VANOILLE"), "Cool Staff Guy," "Glowing Spear Boy."

Honest density accounting. The readings converge on: much denser than casual memory suggests. Peak sections (Cheis interiority, Velinaer anywhere) run one shaped joke per 2–3 sentences; the final duel is ~60% shaped. But the density is unevenly distributed by design: Galar's political/war material runs far drier (situational comedy of manners, little line-wit); grief scenes drop to zero; long battle stretches are functional plain prose; the epilogue is 30–40% shaped. This corrects a claim in llm-tells.md: Avaunt's sentences do not "spend most of their time plainly reporting" in the comic threads. What saves it from machine-uniformity is (a) rest deployed at scene scale — whole sections go flat — and (b) the wit being event- and character-sourced, so it never reads as the same trick twice in a row even at high frequency. Its worst passages are exactly the ones where density is high and mechanism is uniform (jargon-stacked fights).

4. Comedy against darkness

The book earns its jokes by refusing to let them insure anything. Mass death, torture (spikes through palms, severed toes, magical muting), child-slavery undertones, a demon-engineered near-rape setup, a boy deleting his own empathy — all rendered without flinching.

The central tonal trick, consistent across all six readings: horror is narrated straight; the character's response is funny. Cheis's torture is described in unflinching physical detail; her first post-reboot thought is irritation "that whoever had crippled and tortured her had probably stolen her good shoes, too." The comedy reads as coping and competence-under-duress, never as authorial denial that the horror is real.

Three supporting moves:

  • Total joke drop-out as tonal signal. Galar packing to flee and forcing himself to leave his dead wife's painting: no jokes for the whole scene. The marshal's "Robard Pols gave his life to protect his citizens. I want that on the record." The Saurgar Bridge last stand is unironic and mythic. The duel chapter ends, after pages of hacker jargon, on an unadorned "I'm so sorry." The silence is audible because the surrounding register is loud — this is the book's most powerful single device.
  • Horror and joke in the same sentence, neither diluted. "I had to clean bits of her off the ceiling" as a mentor's punchline. "He was alone — completely, irreversibly, and utterly alone, and also naked since his clothes hadn't survived the devastation." The bathetic clause doesn't cancel the grief; it makes it survivable, which is the point.
  • Grief handled obliquely late. The epilogue's darkness goes institutional ("a significant uptake in business for higher-end undertakers") and Cheis's guilt is left as quiet unresolved wreckage under the celebratory wrap-up — an unanswered wedding invitation, never a speech.

The failure edge: occasionally the deflation extends to nameless bystanders ("Cheis didn't really think of bureaucrats as people" over two men falling seven floors) and mass casualties get glib one-liners. It's characterization of Cheis, but repeated it numbs stakes. The book mostly gets away with it; it's the riskiest thing here to borrow.

5. Serial and chapter craft

  • Openings: always in medias res or a one-line character-orienting assessment. "When Galar Kayle awoke, he thought that he was dead." "Linduin Kayle was not, by any stretch of the imagination, a genius." No throat-clearing, no weather.
  • Structure: *** scene breaks crosscut 2–4 POV threads within a chapter, TV-episodic in rhythm. Dramatic irony is the payoff — the reader carries knowledge (the box, the igg, Velinaer's identity) between threads the characters can't. Threads planted early in a chunk pay off inside the same chunk.
  • Pacing: brisk summary compression (weeks of training in a paragraph) alternating with real-time scene for charged beats (the life-or-death rune test, the recruitment speech). Fast violence alternates with slower joke-dense interiority as a relief valve. The finale inverts the pattern: one continuous unbroken set-piece chapter, then a summary-mode epilogue with two real-time domestic vignettes for contrast.
  • Endings: hard buttons, always. Four species: the cliffhanger ("...as Tebes' sword went straight through her chest"), the ominous authorial foreshadow ("It might have cheered her up to know that within seven days, one of them would be [dead]" / "This was, of course, completely incorrect"), the deadpan stinger ("Cheis of Veraleigh 4.0 was no more"), and the tonal-whiplash gut-punch ("I'm so sorry."). Also the suspense-ticker refrain: "The wisp of blackness slithered forward another inch," interleaved through a comic tavern scene to build dread under the jokes.

Note the tension with our rules: Avaunt's chapter endings click shut, hard, and it works — because serial chapters are release units and the click is the subscription hook. The off-center rule in llm-tells.md was written against scene-level callback buttons, which Avaunt mostly doesn't use. Keep both: scenes end off-center; chapter-final beats may click.

6. Exemplar passages (keep these)

"Cheis, who had been to the horizon and left a two-star review, wanted a chocolate cake."

A whole worldview in one balanced sentence: jaded competence plus petty appetite, set against Linduin's wonder. Event-adjacent wit — the joke is her biography.

"Lie here and die: still not a bad plan. Had the benefit of having a 100% success chance with no meaningful side effects."

Genuine near-death despair rendered in her cost-benefit idiom. Humor and pathos in a single beat, no seam. This is Voice B's "dry self-audit" perfected.

"I don't give partial credit. A functional demonstration of how to cheat like a bastard while still solving the problem is nice and all, but you still need to show your work for it to qualify as a general solution."

Mentor warmth with zero sentimentality, and a thematic statement (this book cares about rigor) that never announces itself as theme.

"Please, let me have a second chance at getting you killed? Please, give up your hot sexy girlfriend for me, a dumpy, uncultured psychopath who risks her life out of boredom and self-aggrandizement?"

Self-deprecation that is simultaneously funny and genuinely sad — Bujold-grade emotional honesty about an unglamorous protagonist.

"Velinaer himself, who naturally survived almost completely unscathed, struggled to the top of a tremendous pile of debris and stared out at the wreckage and ruin stretching nearly to the horizon in every direction. In his terminal, a small alert appeared, informing him that his power reserves had dipped to 3.19 ulasovs."

The UI element doing the emotional work: horror via understatement, the system readout as the flattest possible camera.

"It hadn't been a bad life, he supposed. He'd gotten to eat a lot of cereal. Watched his favorite shows. Won that calligraphy competition at nine. Saved the world and/or universe."

Equal-weight listing that smuggles real pathos: a man inventorying his life before planned self-annihilation, in the cadence of a grocery list.

"But, at the end of the day, the facts were inescapable: when all one has is a hammer, one tends to perceive the universe primarily as an environment which allows one to interact with nails. Peace never stood a chance."

The one-aphorism-per-scene ideal: a chapter-closer that is joke, characterization, and theme at once — and stays stuck to its particulars.

"After all, there's no rule that says you can't control a skeleton that someone else is currently using."

Rules-lawyering punchline as narration, immediately after a brutal kill. System-logic as comedy and as genuine tactical resolution — the LitRPG ideal.

"He grabbed rations, a refillable canteen for water, and a traveling cloak..." [→ the painting he cannot take]

Comic-bureaucratic packing efficiency interrupted by grief, with no tonal whiplash because the prose stays controlled and the jokes simply stop.

"'Hello, Umbria,' said Cheis, wishing she were dead. It might have cheered her up to know that within seven days, one of them would be."

The narrator's flash-forward privilege used as both gag and dread in one sentence.

7. Weaknesses — what not to imitate

  1. The conceit as crutch. The tech-jargon magic is brilliant as character voice and deadly as default narration. In fight scenes it stacks three or four invented terms per sentence ("recursively-replicating swarm allocators," "threat-level buffer injection exploit") until the actual choreography is illegible — who's winning becomes unrecoverable under the metaphor. Joke-density destroying scene legibility is the book's most instructive failure.
  2. Static infodumps. Essayistic backstory paragraphs (Tebes' sword, demon taxonomy, the "explanatory pamphlet" gag) stop scenes cold. Delightful individually; momentum-killers in aggregate.
  3. Over-explained jokes and process-narration. Long tinkering passages continue past the point the joke landed. The book sometimes doesn't trust the reader with a device it already sold.
  4. Glibness creep. Deflating every death, including bystanders', numbs stakes ("had not so much perished as questionably existed in the first place"). Funny lines a more restrained author would cut.
  5. Repeated tics. Numbers-as-jokes (12,848%, 5.617e9%), grotesque body horror, italicized profanity for Velinaer — each strong once, each visibly rationed too loosely.
  6. Compressed emotional payoffs. The Galar/Linduin father-son reunion is rushed into banter; the epilogue crams relationship resolutions into run-on plot-report sentences — efficient but un-witnessed. Big feelings need scene-time even in a fast serial.
  7. Visible coincidence gears. Random-sabotage tragedy (the thrown rock) used twice in quick succession; Tebes' humanizing paragraph arriving one page before his death reads as authorial cleverness, not earned characterization.
  8. Baggy sentences. Comma-spliced pile-ups in battle and body-horror passages dilute punchlines instead of sharpening them.
  9. Stock romance mechanics. The love-triangle beats are the least fresh material in the book — the comic and system material carries them, not vice versa.

8. Lessons for our voice (cross-referenced)

Already captured — confirmed by the whole book:

  • The self-image/reality pivot, sentence-level wit, teeth, character-flavoured narration (avaunt-influence.md §1–6) all hold up across 84k words; they're not prologue-only effects.
  • Voice A's "at emotional peaks the narrator shuts up" (voices.md) is exactly Avaunt's best move, validated repeatedly (the painting, the marshal, "I'm so sorry.").
  • Event-wit over ornament-wit (llm-tells.md): confirmed as the reason Avaunt's density doesn't read as machine sparkle. Its jokes are made of plot, character biography, and system logic; almost none are transplantable decorations.

  • llm-tells.md's claim that Avaunt's "sentences spend most of their time plainly reporting" is wrong for the comic threads — peak density is 1 joke per 2–3 sentences. Add to the notes:

  1. Diegetic system voice. The single most important LitRPG lesson here: make the system vocabulary one character's cognitive idiom, not a world-overlay. Cheis built her magic-as-software framework herself (it's in her childhood diary); Galar and the villagers experience magic as numinous mystery. This gives you register-clash comedy, hard-system stakes, and worldbuilding contrast for free — and it means the system talk characterizes rather than gamifies. Our System should be experienced differently by different POVs.
  2. The UI readout as emotional instrument. A status line after devastation ("power reserves had dipped to 3.19 ulasovs") is the flattest camera available — use System text for horror-via-understatement and for punchlines, not just bookkeeping.
  3. System logic must resolve plot. Avaunt's conceit pays off because rules-lawyering wins fights and a restart command performs a mythic resurrection ("no rule that says you can't control a skeleton someone else is currently using"). If the system never solves anything the jokes are stickers.
  4. Legibility ceiling on system prose. Hard rule from Avaunt's failure mode: in any action beat, the physical event must be recoverable by a reader who skips every jargon term. If who's-winning depends on parsing invented vocabulary, cut jargon until it doesn't.
  5. Tonal ecosystem across POV threads. Avaunt extends our dialogue-ecosystem rule to structure: different POV threads run at different wit densities (Velinaer loud, Galar nearly dry). If we run multiple threads, give them different registers rather than one voice everywhere.
  6. Horror straight, response funny. Codify the coping-not-denial rule: never joke about the atrocity in narration; let the character's practical, petty, or deflecting response carry the comedy ("probably stolen her good shoes, too"). And police the edge: named characters' deaths and bystanders' deaths don't get the deflation treatment by default.
  7. Equal-weight listing for pathos. The cereal-list move — grief itemized in trivial cadence — is a repeatable technique that stays inside our aphorism budget because it's built from the character's particulars.
  8. The suspense-ticker refrain. A short repeated dread-sentence interleaved through a comic scene ("The wisp of blackness slithered forward another inch") builds stakes under jokes without changing register. Cheap, effective, underused elsewhere.
  9. Narrator flash-forward as licensed aphorism-slot. "By the next sunset..." / "This was, of course, completely incorrect." One per chapter maximum; it doubles as the hook.
  10. Ration the signature conceit. Whatever our equivalent of magic-as-software is, Avaunt shows both that one conceit can carry a whole book and precisely how it curdles: when it appears in every paragraph of interiority, when it stacks terms in action, when the same joke shape (big number, gross-out, profane italic) recurs unmutated. Track conceit-appearances per scene the way we track em-dashes.
  11. Give reunions room. When converging threads finally meet, resist compressing the payoff into banter. Avaunt's father-son reunion is the cautionary example: the crosscutting structure spent chapters earning a scene it then skipped.
