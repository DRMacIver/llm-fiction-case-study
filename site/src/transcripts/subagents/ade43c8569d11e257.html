The people. redacted's grandmother's people are the Ell folk, of the low silt-and-saltmarsh country at the seaward outfall of the fen basin — the Ellings — roughly seventy miles east-north-east of Fenholt, three counties from the vale, where the freshwater peat gives out into silt, sea-bank and saltern. They are the same stock, language and pantheon as everyone else in the lowlands; the difference is the household form and the god that goes with it.

They live on holms: dry-point islands and old sea-banks, one extended household to a holm, twelve to forty people. Livelihood is salt off the marsh, wildfowl, eels, reed and sedge, summer grazing on the levels when the water falls, turf and peat cut from the fen edge inland, and coasting boats. The sea takes people — surge, tide, boat, and the bad water offshore — in every generation, in numbers, and the great communal obligation is the bank: the sea-banks and marsh drains, which want days of work forever and pay nothing back except that the ground stays there. Land is not held individually. The holm is held by the household, and the household is the legal person. "redacted" is not a family surname; it is the name of a holm-house, and redacted carries a place with people in it.

2. The religion. Not a separate church. The Ellings keep the ordinary round — parish priest, blessings out of a book, the church's major gods — and additionally hold a minor household deity, the Doorwife (formal name in the church's list, almost never used: Anneth).

What it holds. The Doorwife does not see and cannot be prayed to silently. redacted sentence: unpublished prose What binds is speech before witnesses. Writing is the copy; the saying is the act; the reading-back is what makes the copy true.

What it asks (all of it costs):

  • The saying. Anything that changes who owes what is said at the household's meal, with the door hooked open, and outsiders on the ground may hear. Grievances are included, and are on a clock: a grievance not said by the third supper is discharged, and may never be raised again. You confront on schedule or you lose the right to.
  • The letting-go. Any party to any agreement may say at any meal, once, I'm out of it, and is out from that moment. No reason may be asked and none may be given. Nobody may argue, and nobody may be shamed for going. The cost falls on the leaver: they go with what they came with and their share of the year, and the house is not theirs afterwards.
  • The bank. Every adult over eighteen owes days on the sea-bank and the drains, counted, and read out by name at the quarter, days worked and days not. This is the religion's actual sacrament: the god's work is the upkeep of the ground everyone stands on.
  • The dead. At a death the household reads out every agreement the dead person stood in and dissolves each one aloud, by name, in order. Grief has a form, and the form is bookkeeping.
  • The shut door. The one hard prohibition: nothing agreed behind a shut door binds anyone. A shut door means this is not being decided. Sex, quarrels, illness, weeping, dying go behind it and have no standing. Decisions come out to the table.
  • The Doorwife's night, the last night of Frostmonth: every household sits from dark to midnight with the door hooked open whatever the weather, and everything unsaid in the year gets said. It is cold, long, dreaded, and nobody misses it.

Small practices a character can mention in passing. Every Ell door has a hook to hold it open, and an Ell-raised person unhooks a door to mean not now. A pinch of salt is set out on the table at a saying and returned to the pot after — marsh people waste nothing. Nobody sits with their back to the door while a thing is being said. Said and written is the ordinary Ell phrase for settled. Doorwife hear it is what they say where anyone else would say God willing, and it marks an Ell speaker instantly.

3. Household structure. The holm-house: two elders (the ones who sit at the door), their generation's partners, working adults, children, and taken-in people — widowed neighbours, orphans, incomers who asked and were said in. Children are in the charge of the household, not of a couple. Paternity is known and is not load-bearing, because there is no individual inheritance to route through it: the holm passes to the household, and a death removes nothing but agreements. Household names descend through the mother's holm. One office matters: the hand — the household member who writes the agreements and reads them back, holds the book, and strikes lines. It is a job, held for decades, and it is taught to a child young.

4. Marriages and agreements. Marriage is not a separate kind of thing. It is an agreement, and the only distinctions are who witnesses and how long the supper is.

  • A two-agreement: two people, said at the table, written, ordinary.
  • A house-agreement: three or more parties, or any agreement bringing a person into the household. It requires, all of it, or it is not one: said aloud at the door with the door hooked open before the whole household; written by the hand, who is a member and not a party; read back the same night; marked by every party and by the two who sat; and a supper the household provides afterwards. The supper is not celebration. It is the proof — the household paid for it, so the household can be called to remember it.
  • Dissolution: as the letting-go, above. The hand strikes the line through, legibly, with the date and the leaving party's name, and does not erase it, so the run of years reads true.
  • What cannot be agreed: a thing you cannot be let out of. They have a word for that other thing and it is not a kind word. Nearest translation: a holding. The word is only ever spoken at the Doorwife's night, at the door, and only to those of age to stand there — which is eighteen, the Choosing.

Multiple partners are common — perhaps a third of Ell adults stand in a house-agreement with more than one — and are a consequence of the household form and the drowning demography, not a tenet. Nobody preaches it. Inland it is the thing people have heard of and get wrong.
