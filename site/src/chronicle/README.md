# Chronicle

This is a day by day account of the ten days David MacIver spent building "How to Save the World" with Claude Code, from first setting the premise on 24 August 2026 to finishing the draft on 2 September. It is reconstructed from git history and from the session transcripts published elsewhere on this site, so it only knows what committed files and recorded conversations show. Where the record is thin, the entry is short rather than padded.

## How to read this

Each day has its own page, headed by a one-line summary of what happened. Where a day's work happened inside a single recorded session, the page links to that session's transcript. Several days share a session, because David's sessions often ran past midnight, and where that happens the same transcript link appears on more than one day's page. A few days combine two or three shorter sessions.

Nothing here goes beyond what the published chapters and the book's own blurb already say. Character names other than Edwin Moss and Peri Millbrook, later plot content, and anything past the published scenes are left out, under the same redaction rules the transcripts use.

## The arc of it

The first day and a half were spent not writing fiction at all, but arguing with the model about how it should sound. David had it read published web fiction for register, study a couple of well-regarded webfiction books for craft lessons, and build a list of "LLM tells", the reflexive habits (certain adverbs, tidy three-part lists, decorative endings) that mark prose as machine-written. Most of that list stuck for the rest of the project. Once a voice was chosen, canon files, a magic system, and a five-book series plan went up fast, and drafting began.

Then came the pattern that repeats through the whole project: draft, get flagged, throw away, redo. A batch of exemplar chapters were deleted early on as scaffolding, which turned out to break the voice, because later drafting sessions had nothing concrete to imitate and drifted back toward generic fantasy prose. They were restored. A citation-checking script found that background documents describing the world were fabricating quotations from the manuscript that had never been written, dozens of them, and those had to be found and fixed by hand. Most dramatically, a full second act of some sixty-five scenes was drafted, reviewed, and then rejected outright on 30 August for contamination, meaning the new prose had leaned too hard on older discarded drafts and had to start again from a clean brief.

Underneath the drafting, David kept building small pieces of infrastructure as the need for them became obvious: a tool to check for repeated phrases across the whole manuscript, a local web app for leaving inline comments on chapters, a script to keep a personal e-reader in sync with the latest draft, and eventually a full Royal Road publishing pipeline, built by driving the site's own upload forms in a scripted browser.

By the last day covered here the full draft was finished, across three acts, and the opening chapters were already live on Royal Road on a daily release schedule. What readers have so far, the first forty scenes grouped into chapters, is the surface of a process that also produced several complete drafts nobody will ever read, and a set of house rules about prose habits that got argued over, tightened, and occasionally reversed as new problems turned up.
