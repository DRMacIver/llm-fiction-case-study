# Farming facts and the chapter notes

The book's magic system sits on top of a rule that has nothing to do with magic: every agronomic, soil, livestock, weather, or drainage fact that appears on the page has to be a real fact, drawn from actual pre-industrial or modern agricultural science and practice, and magic is only allowed to modify a real farming practice in response to a real problem farmers actually have. Frost on blossom, a heap of compost going airless, a sick ewe, finding water underground: the spell exists because the problem exists, and the spell has to still cost the labour it saves, not remove the work outright. A compost cantrip that finished a heap in an hour with none of the risks a fast heap runs in reality would break the rule, whatever the story needed it for.

David set this constraint directly, and it functions less as worldbuilding flavour than as a check the model has to actually pass: if a drafter is unsure whether a claimed fact is real, it doesn't go on the page. The project keeps a running glossary of specialist farming terms, recording each one's real-world basis and which chapter first explains it, precisely so a term is never re-explained once it's been handled and never left unexplained when a reader genuinely needs the grounding. A dedicated farming-check skill runs at the editing stage of every chapter, extracting every claim and specialist term and checking each against the glossary and against real mechanism, not just plausibility.

## Where the explanation lives

Explaining a specialist farming term on the page happens through the notes at the foot of a chapter, a Royal Road convention where the writer adds a short real-world aside beneath the story text. These are distinct from the notes at the top of a chapter, which are David's own words and which the rules forbid the model to touch at all. These notes are bound to a specific written persona: an agriculture-science postgraduate, more scientist than farmer, who has done real farm work (lambing, digging worms out of soil, tending a sick animal) without ever having carried the full responsibility of running a farm themselves, and who gets genuinely excited sharing the real science behind whatever the chapter just touched on. David's original brief was blunt: the first attempt at these notes was boring, and what he wanted instead was someone who mostly kept the notes tightly relevant but occasionally couldn't resist a digression.

The persona is written down as its own file with an autobiographical log, tracking every claim the notes have made about the author's own life (which orchard, which river, which thermometer), so a later note can't casually contradict an earlier one. Like the prose voice itself, this persona was found through exemplars rather than specification alone: a set of accepted note drafts get read before writing any new one, because notes drafted from the rule file alone came out lifeless in the same way rules-only prose drafting did. The notes are budgeted too, one longer digression or a couple of shorter notes per chapter, and they're barred from ever explaining the story, the magic system, or the world's institutions. Their only subject is the real-world fact behind whatever the story just needed grounding.
